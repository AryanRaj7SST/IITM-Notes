<TopicSection id="command-economy" title="3. Command economy: how it works">

<KeyIdea>
In a command economy a central authority sets rules for what, for whom, when and how much to produce.
</KeyIdea>

> **Definition (from the lecture):** A command (socialist, centrally dictated or directed) economy is a rule-based system in which the rules are made by central authorities, the government, which decides what to produce, to whom, when and how.

> **Plain language:** Like code where one authority writes the if/else rules and you cannot add or change any rule yourself.

- Data science analogy: a rule-based system like conditions in code. If the condition is correct, proceed with the command; otherwise stop. Like a company employee, you cannot add one more loop without permission and cannot go beyond the boundary.
- **Mango example:** the government says if demand is more than 100 kg per day, produce 50 kg per day more; otherwise hold the production.
- **T-shirt example:** if demand is 100 t-shirts per day, produce 50 more t-shirts; otherwise hold production.

```text
if demand per day is above 100 kg:
    produce 50 kg per day more
else:
    hold the production
```

**Q&A: does the rule mean they already produce and make 50 more?**
- It is a rule system. Example: a company sells toys and has a stock of about 200 toys, selling nearly 100 per day. The government says to produce 50 more to fulfil the inventory. Otherwise one day everything is sold and the next day there is nothing to sell.
- The example is only to show that decisions come from the central authority.

<CheckBadge reason="Speech-to-text gave 'software toy'; the instructor said soft toy (or stuffed toy)." />

- Demand and everything is governed by the central government. If it says produce 50 more, you must, even if you say you only have manpower for 25 more. The government plays the part of a **dictator** over day-to-day activities and production.

</TopicSection>

<TopicSection id="command-qa" title="4. Command economy: Q&A, examples and drawbacks">

<KeyIdea>
Command economies give tight control but are rigid, brittle and weak on motivation and innovation.
</KeyIdea>

**Q&A: are the social and moral questions around demand (for example liquor) part of economics?**
- At country level it is macroeconomics, not micro. GDP, unemployment, employment and surveys (the instructor names NRC) are macroeconomics, because the decision is for a larger set of states or a country.
- Economics also covers human behaviour and human decisions.

<CheckBadge reason="The instructor says 'NRC' as an example of a survey; kept as transcribed." />

**Economics as allocation of resources**
- Because of scarcity we cannot meet unlimited human needs, so we must allocate limited resources to fulfil needs with maximum satisfaction.
- Example: if I want an Audi but have no money, I look for a car with similar functions, such as a Mahindra with nearly 50 to 80 percent of the features, which still gives satisfaction.

**Examples of command economy**
- Before the 1990s many things in India were governed by the government; some sectors still show this dominance.
- The Soviet Union was totally governed by central authorities. Hitler is another example: whatever decision he wanted, he took.

**Drawbacks**
- No willingness to compete and no innovation, because of the rules.
- Lack of motivation, because the same salary is paid to all persons.
- No flexibility: like being locked in a room doing whatever orders you are given.
- The system is rigid and therefore brittle: when a change is needed it cannot adapt and fails.
- Example: **demonetization**. The money had to be changed immediately, and when implemented immediately the whole scenario was chaotic.

<TopicSection id="free-market" title="5. Free market (capitalist) economy">

<KeyIdea>
In a free market economy companies and individuals take the decisions, with little or no government role.
</KeyIdea>

> **Definition (from the lecture):** In a free market (capitalist) economy, individual companies or their authorities have the power to take decisions. It is not bounded by government rules, and the government has no role in making decisions.

> **Plain language:** The owner decides prices, offers and innovations, and the main task is to survive in a competitive market.

- **Reliance, 2003:** it launched a phone when tariffs for outgoing and incoming calls were very high. Making incoming calls free was the company's own decision, with no hand of the government. It caused a revolution in telecom, and in one day nearly 3.5 million people took a Reliance plan because it was very cheap.
- To survive, competitors had to match prices. Airtel and other providers reduced tariffs, because customers were switching to Reliance. Company authorities took that decision.
- **Swiggy:** customised offers applicable to you only. To whom to give an offer is decided by company authorities, and the government cannot say why. A coupon or refund from customer care is also a local company decision.
- **Mobile phones:** Apple, Samsung and Android makers decide prices by features and what innovations to bring, with very minimal or no government interference.
- **Local vegetable vendors:** for 1 kg the price is fixed, for 10 kg they negotiate. The shop owner decides.

</TopicSection>

<TopicSection id="mixed-economy" title="6. Mixed economy">

<KeyIdea>
A mixed economy is a free market with government restrictions in selected areas, such as social welfare, safety and customer trust.
</KeyIdea>

> **Definition (from the lecture):** A mixed economy is one where the government also has a stake or decision power in some sectors of a business, such as in terms of social welfare.

> **Plain language:** Companies are free, but the government closes certain gates when people are hurt or money is at risk.

**Example: the 10-minute delivery**
- Last year delivery apps competed to claim delivery in 10 minutes. That was a company decision with no government role, so it falls under the capitalistic model.
- Accidents increased and the work-life balance of delivery partners deteriorated, because everybody rushed.
- The Supreme Court made a rule: no company can claim or assure 10-minute delivery, and the health of delivery partners must be taken care of. This is government interference in a capitalist economy.

**India as a mixed economy**
- Public sector companies and banks exist. In banking, the RBI (owned by the government) fixes the interest percentage and regulates account opening, for example KYC. Without KYC you cannot open an account.
- Reasons for restrictions: reduce fraud, ensure stability in business sectors and maintain customer trust.
- School-days story: a bank took a lot of money from customers and flew away, and its employees stopped answering calls because there were no policies.
- UPI transactions are secure because NPCI keeps a hold on them; otherwise many fake transactions would happen and money would be lost.
- Chart idea: within the free market there are gates, and some are controlled by the government. Trading has SEBI regulation. Delivery was a gate the government closed. Swiggy, Zomato and Amazon wallets need KYC, with strict boundaries to protect customers' money.

<TopicSection id="open-closed" title="8. Open vs closed economy">

<KeyIdea>
An open economy exchanges trade, technology and investment with others; a closed economy keeps everything inside its boundary.
</KeyIdea>

> **Open economy (from the lecture):** Anybody can come, invest and trade, through free trade or a nominal tax rate, and technology, resources and investment are exchanged. Example: the BrahMos missile, built on a collaboration between India and Russia.

> **Closed economy (from the lecture):** Nothing goes out of the system. Innovations stay inside, with no exchange across the boundary.

- Example of a closed economy: North Korea, which seems not to share anything with other countries and not even take raw materials from them.
- Drawback: technology is more expensive and takes longer, because everything must be studied, researched and developed, and may or may not succeed. Like a locked room where you can only innovate inside the room.

</TopicSection>

<TopicSection id="social-science" title="10. Why economics is a social science">

<KeyIdea>
Results in economics vary with place and people, unlike deterministic science, though economics uses optimization, a pure science tool.
</KeyIdea>

> **Definition (from the lecture):** In science, results are universally valid and deterministic: they do not change with geographical location, age group or gender. In social science, parameters change with geography and human factors.

> **Plain language:** Science gives one answer everywhere; economics gives different answers for different people and places.

- Science examples: $1 + 1 = 2$ holds in Chennai, the US or France. Newton's law with an apple falling: acceleration due to gravity $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$ is the same in every location, so forces for the same parameters in the US and India are equal.

<CheckBadge reason="Speech-to-text gave 'isolation'; the instructor said acceleration due to gravity." />

- Social science examples:
  - With nearly 61 students in the session, asking each for an example of microeconomics or a free market economy gives multiple different answers, depending on mindset and knowledge.
  - Rice production in Tamil Nadu, Punjab, Assam or Gujarat differs in quality and process; cropping in Tamil Nadu is not the same as in Punjab.
- Plot idea: deterministic results ($1+1=2$, $2+2=4$) stay the same everywhere. A survey on whether the myth that economics is only related to money is true gives a **scatter plot**, because answers change with location, age and gender.
- So there is no single concrete answer for everything, which is why it is a social science. Within it, **optimization**, a pure science, is used to allocate resources.

</TopicSection>

<TopicSection id="toothbrush" title="11. Economics as a system: the toothbrush case">

<KeyIdea>
Customer demand creates innovation; economics studies, analyzes and optimizes a system, which gives models.

- Toothbrush example: why was there a need for a toothbrush in the early 1990s?

**Business link**
- When you get 6 months or 3 to 4 years of company data and do a product analysis, check whether the company works only in traditional ways. Without advancement the customer retention rate will drop and customers go to alternatives.
- Continuous feedback is very important to see whether customers drop or increase and whether the product suits them.

**Toothbrush timeline**
- 1990s: normal toothbrushes.
- 2000s: zig-zag bristles, flexible necks, based on customer inputs.
- 21st century: electronic toothbrushes.

<CheckBadge reason="The instructor says '20th century' for the second stage; chronologically it is the turn of the 2000s." />

- Economic point: whether you have money for an electronic toothbrush or use a traditional one creates demand, and prices vary with technological advancement.

</TopicSection>

<TopicSection id="three-elements" title="12. Production, distribution and consumption">

<KeyIdea>
Economics is the study of the production-to-consumption chain, optimizing limited resources against demand.
</KeyIdea>

- A company such as Oral-B starts production based on customer feedback, then distribution: vendors and suppliers, then the market or shop, then customers (consumption).
- The three elements of economics are **Production**, **Distribution** and **Consumption**.
- As an economist the goal is to study how to optimize resources. Raw material is limited, but consumption demand must be fulfilled, so you optimize so that limited raw material produces enough to meet demand.
- The study of this chain is economics. The instructor stresses that money is not the topic here, but the process of optimization or allocation of resources.
- To optimize production, study relationships between variables, for example how raw material changes with time (linearly, non-linearly or fluctuating a lot). These relationships give models and theories to satisfy unlimited wants with limited resources.

</TopicSection>

<TopicSection id="ramu" title="15. Case study 1: Ramu's tea and coffee stall">

<KeyIdea>
Fix the budget constraint, then allocate what remains to find quantities and profit.
</KeyIdea>

| Item | Tea ($T$) | Filter coffee ($F$) |
|---|---|---|
| Production cost per cup | ₹6 | ₹12 |
| Profit per cup | ₹4 | ₹6 |

- Constraint: daily raw material budget of ₹1,200 (milk, coffee powder, tea powder and so on).

<CheckBadge reason="The instructor first uses C for tea in parts of the equation, then switches between T and C; normalised to T for tea and F for filter coffee." />

$$6T + 12F = 1200$$

- Coffee costs twice as much as tea to make, so there is a trade-off (opportunity cost): more coffee means fewer cups of tea to stay within ₹1,200.
- **Scenario:** a workshop at IIT Madras gives Ramu a fixed order of 40 filter coffees ($F = 40$). He can take the order or refuse and continue regular sales. His regular tea customers would be disappointed and go to another shop, so he takes the order and uses the remaining budget for tea.

**Question 1: budget left and cups of tea after 40 coffees**
- Start from the constraint: $6T + 12F = 1200$.
- Put $F = 40$: $6T = 1200 - 12(40)$.
- Coffee spends $12 \times 40 = 480$, so $6T = 1200 - 480 = 720$ (budget left ₹720).
- $T = \dfrac{720}{6} = 120$ cups of tea.

**Question 2: total net profit (objective function)**

$$\text{Total Profit} = 4T + 6F$$

- $\text{Total Profit} = 4(120) + 6(40) = 480 + 240 = ₹720$.

> The same allocation idea applies in a project: for example, with a limited budget for both an app and a PowerBI dashboard, you reallocate resources to fulfil both orders.

**Q&A: what if regular tea demand is 150 cups but only 120 can be made?**
- He fulfils the 120 cups. He may miss some customers, which is fine, because he still serves most of them and uses his full ₹1,200 without wasting milk.

<AddedByTutor>

</AddedByTutor>

<TopicSection id="ppf" title="16. The PPF idea and the Ramu Q&A">

<KeyIdea>
On the curve means full use of resources, under it means waste, beyond it needs extra resources.
</KeyIdea>

> **Definition (from the lecture):** The Production Possibility Frontier (PPF) curve shows combinations of what you can produce using your resources.

> **Plain language:** The curve is the line of full use; the budget line in Ramu's stall is an example.

| Position | Meaning |
|---|---|
| On the curve | 100 percent use of resources, following the constraint equation; if tea goes down, coffee picks up |
| Under the curve (undershooting) | Resources not properly utilised, so efficiency and profit go down |
| Beyond the curve (overshooting) | Needs extra resources, such as more labor or machines, to meet the demand |

**Q&A: if maximum tea capacity is 200 cups and there are 200 regular tea customers, how do we decide on the 40-coffee order by profit?**
- If 100 percent of resources go to 200 cups of tea alone, you are at the vertical axis intercept (only tea, zero coffee).
- To meet extra demand without cutting tea, you must go beyond the curve: expand capacity by hiring more workers or buying machines.

<TopicSection id="farm" title="17. Case study 2: organic farming (Basmati rice and wheat)">

<KeyIdea>
A water limit and a minimum Basmati area decide how much wheat can be grown and what the profit is.
</KeyIdea>

| Item | Basmati rice ($B$) | Wheat ($W$) |
|---|---|---|
| Profit per acre | ₹15,000 | ₹8,000 |
| Water per acre per day | 500 litres | 200 litres |

- **Constraint 1 (water):** water comes from tube wells, and the government limits use to at most 10,000 litres per day.
- The instructor first wrote 20 litres for wheat and corrected it immediately to 200 litres.

$$500B + 200W = 10000$$

- **Constraint 2 (soil fertility, mixed farming):** to maintain fertility and nitrogen content, at least 5 acres of Basmati rice must be grown, so $B \ge 5$ (taken as $B = 5$ in the solution). For the rest of the land it is the farmer's choice.

**Q&A: explain the second constraint again.**
- Cultivating different crops to increase soil fertility is called mixed farming. It is a fixed requirement that out of his total land he grows at least 5 acres of Basmati rice.

**Question 1: how many acres of wheat can he grow?**
- Water used by 5 acres of Basmati: $5 \times 500 = 2500$ litres.
- Remaining water: $10000 - 2500 = 7500$ litres.
- Wheat at 200 litres per acre: $W = \dfrac{7500}{200} = 37.5$ acres.

**Question 2: total profit**

$$\text{Total Profit} = 15000B + 8000W$$

- $= 15000(5) + 8000(37.5) = 75000 + 300000 = ₹3{,}75{,}000$ (₹3.75 lakhs).

- Remark: even for large Excel sheets in a project that allocate budgets across projects, the concept is the same. First write your constraint equation, identify the fixed budgets you cannot move, then allocate the variable parameters to see the profit.

<TopicSection id="market-production" title="19. Market and production: goods and services">

<KeyIdea>
A market is where commodities are exchanged; production turns raw materials into usable goods (visible) and services (invisible).
</KeyIdea>

> **Market (from the lecture):** A place where people interact to exchange commodities (goods and services) to fulfil needs. In return there is a flow of money or resources (for example barter of tea for snacks, or working and trading for profit).

> **Production (from the lecture):** The process of converting raw materials into usable (consumable) items that fulfil the demand of consumers.

> **Plain language:** You cannot use raw material directly, so production changes it into something people can use.

- You cannot eat raw tea leaves for milk tea or wear raw cotton. Raw cotton becomes t-shirts, shirts or sarees; tea leaves become milk tea or black tea.

| Outcome of production | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Visible (tangible): **goods** | Items you can see, touch and store |
| Invisible (intangible): **services** | Outcomes you can only experience and cannot store |

- **Milkman example:** the milk is the visible part (a good); the delivery from his home to yours is the invisible part (a service).

</TopicSection>

<TopicSection id="factors" title="20. Factors of production">

<KeyIdea>
Land, labor, physical capital and entrepreneurship are needed for every production activity.
</KeyIdea>

Every production activity, goods-based or service-based, needs four factors:

| Factor | Meaning and examples |
|---|---|
| Land | The basic physical requirement: finalising, buying or leasing land or a building for a company or shop |
| Labor | Workers or skilled laborers to build infrastructure and run day-to-day operations across departments |
| Physical capital: fixed capital | Long-term investments made at the start or for multiple years: land or buildings, leasing an office for 10 years, a production or assembly line, machines, power generators or powerhouses |
| Physical capital: working capital | Short-term, frequent or monthly operational expenses: salaries, monthly electricity and water bills, cloud storage subscriptions, replacing machine tools that wear out every 3 to 4 months |
| Entrepreneurship (human capital) | People who organise, take risks and manage the other resources: CEOs, C-suite executives, senior managers, R&D heads, data scientists handling projects, HODs in an institute who propose budgets and allocate funds to labs |

<TopicSection id="sectors" title="21. The five economic sectors">

<KeyIdea>
Economic activity is split into primary, secondary, tertiary, quaternary and quinary sectors.

To see how much each activity contributes to the Indian economy, activities are classified into five sectors, illustrated by making and serving tea at home:

| Sector | Workforce | Tea analogy | Meaning | Share of India's GDP |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary | Red-collar | Collecting the raw materials (tea leaves, milk, sugar, masala) | Root of the sectors: extracting and collecting raw materials and natural resources (agriculture, mining) | About 17 percent |
| Secondary | Blue-collar | Mixing milk, tea leaves and sugar in a pan and heating on the stove | Production and processing of raw materials into finished goods (raw material quality checks, cleaning, machine processing, manufacturing) | Nearly 27 percent, of which 14 percent is manufacturing of visible goods |

- Over 50 percent of the economy depends on the tertiary sector. This is why the government prioritises fuel allocation to keep logistics running.

<TopicSection id="sector-qa" title="22. Q&A: dominance of the tertiary sector">

<KeyIdea>
Depending heavily on services raises dependence on others and competition, so a balanced mix of sectors is needed.
</KeyIdea>

**Q: how does one sector dominating (tertiary at 54 percent, manufacturing lower) affect development, and is it good or bad?**
- Refineries (for example in Assam) and pharmaceutical or medical device plants (for example in Himachal Pradesh) sit in specific regions, so massive logistics is needed to supply products across the country.
- India does not manufacture as much domestically; many Indian companies take consultancy and service projects. Example: Airbus gives numerical simulation projects to India while the manufacturing is done abroad. So manufacturing contributes less and services more.
- **Is it good or bad?**
  - Relying on services means depending on others for physical products, and service businesses face huge competition because anyone can replicate a service. Example: IIT Madras launched the first online BS degree, and IIT Roorkee and IIT Guwahati soon launched similar programs.
  - If you manufacture a unique physical product, you can build a monopoly where customers have to come to you.
  - Ultimately an economy needs a balanced mix of sectors depending on conditions and products.

</TopicSection>
