<TopicSection id="recap" title="1. Recap: needs, wants, desire and the economic problem">

<KeyIdea>
Wants are unlimited and resources are scarce, so scarce resources must be allocated well; this is the economic problem.
</KeyIdea>

- Previous video: economics defined as the science of constrained choices.
- A need is essential for survival and well-being. Once basic needs are satisfied, people start thinking of wants, which enhance the quality of life. Desire differs from a want by a form of affordability.
- Macroeconomics and microeconomics were discussed briefly.
- Human beings have unlimited, infinite or insatiable wants, but the resources required are finite (scarce). Because we do not have all that we want, scarce resources must be allocated well. This is the **economic problem**, and economics is all about dealing with it.
- If human wants were finite and resources unlimited, there would be no economic problem and no economics. This is not the case.

</TopicSection>

<TopicSection id="demand" title="2. Demand">

<KeyIdea>
Demand is desire backed by the ability and willingness to pay.
</KeyIdea>

> **Definition (from the lecture):** Demand is desire backed by the ability and willingness to pay.

> **Plain language:** Wanting something is not enough; you must also be able and ready to pay for it.

$$\text{Demand} = \text{Desire} + \text{Ability to pay} + \text{Willingness to pay}$$

- Desire is mostly based on emotion. A person may want to purchase a product but lack the funds; that is a desire.
- **Example:** a person from a low-income group wants to buy a luxury car. The company cannot consider this demand, because the person has no purchasing power and lacks the ability to pay the price.
- Anyone without purchasing power cannot demand a good or service. Think of demand as something that comes with purchasing power.
- Demand and supply are covered in later weeks. This video gives the basics; the live sessions by Dr. Aditya Chandel cover practical things, problems and discussions.

</TopicSection>

<TopicSection id="micro-macro" title="4. Microeconomics and macroeconomics in detail">

<KeyIdea>
Micro looks at the society bottom-up (consumers and businesses); macro looks at it top-down (the economy as a whole).
</KeyIdea>

| | Microeconomics | Macroeconomics |
|---|---|---|
| Word origin | Greek micro, meaning small | Greek macro, meaning larger |
| Focus | Smaller parts of the economy: individual consumers, businesses and their financial decisions | The economy as a whole, the larger part of the economy |
| View of society | Bottom-up | Top-down |
| Examples | IPL franchises; a consumer who works more and has less leisure; impact of tariffs or geopolitical tensions on individual consumers and businesses | A government allocating more budget to education and less to infrastructure; impact of tariffs or geopolitical tensions on the Indian economy (inflation, GDP, employment rate); a government steering the economy by increasing or decreasing taxes |

- **IPL example (micro):** there are 10 teams (RCB, CSK, Delhi Capitals, Punjab Kings and others). Every team wants the best batsmen, bowler, all-rounder, wicket-keeper and captain, but has constraints on squad size and budget, a cap or ceiling.
- **Geopolitical example (macro):** the recent geopolitical tensions disrupted the supply chain, so the fuel supply is affected in India and other countries.
- Both topics are very detailed; this video gives a preliminary understanding.
- Constrained choice applies in both micro and macro, and the decision maker must make the best decision subject to the constraints. The impact of policies on the whole economy (inflation, GDP, employment rate) is macro; the impact on consumers, businesses and employees is micro.

</TopicSection>

<TopicSection id="scarcity" title="6. Scarcity">

<KeyIdea>
Unlimited wants meet scarce resources, which creates an imbalance called scarcity.
</KeyIdea>

> **Definition (from the lecture):** Scarcity means short supply.

> **Plain language:** There is not enough of something to meet everyone's wants.

- Theoretically, human wants (needs, demands) are unlimited, while the resources required to meet them are always scarce. One side has more, the other has scarcity, so there is an imbalance.
- Resources that are scarce: land, capital, labor, production, technology, knowledge, time and natural resources.
- If wants were finite and resources unlimited, there would be no need to study economics and no economic problem. This is never the case; there is always scarcity.
- Demand comes with purchasing power, so demand is a desire plus the ability and willingness to pay.

</TopicSection>

<TopicSection id="utility" title="8. Utility">

<KeyIdea>
Utility is the satisfaction a consumer gets from consuming goods or services, and it differs between people.
</KeyIdea>

> **Definition (from the lecture):** In economics, utility refers to the satisfaction the consumer gets upon consumption of goods and/or services. It influences economic decisions.

> **Plain language:** Utility is how much satisfaction you get from using or consuming something.

- **Coat example:** two people buy a coat (jacket). It is very unlikely they get the same utility.
  - Person A lives in a colder climate and uses it more frequently, so the utility is higher.
  - Person B lives in a warmer climate and uses it less frequently, so the utility is lower.

</TopicSection>

<TopicSection id="marginality" title="9. Marginality and marginal utility">

<KeyIdea>
People decide bit by bit; marginal utility is the extra satisfaction from one more unit.
</KeyIdea>

- **Marginality:** people do not make economic decisions all in one go, but bit by bit.

> **Definition (from the lecture):** Marginal utility is the incremental increase in utility derived from the consumption of one additional (extra) unit.

> **Plain language:** How much more satisfied you get from one more unit. "Marginal" means by a margin.

$$\text{MU} = \text{incremental increase in utility from one additional unit consumed}$$

</TopicSection>

<TopicSection id="dmu" title="10. Law of diminishing marginal utility">

<KeyIdea>
As consumption increases, the marginal utility decreases and can even turn negative.
</KeyIdea>

> **Definition (from the lecture):** As consumption increases, the marginal utility associated with it decreases.

> **Plain language:** Each extra unit satisfies you a bit less than the one before.

- **Ice cream example:** put quantity of ice creams consumed on the x axis and utility (satisfaction) on the y axis.
  - First ice cream: utility is the highest, the greatest pleasure.
  - Second ice cream (assuming the same size): the satisfaction is not the same and comes down, because you have already tasted it, are tired of the same thing, or feel full.
  - Overconsumption (you are full): marginal utility can turn **negative** (negative utility).
- **Pizza example:** when hungry, the first slice gives the maximum pleasure. The second slice gives satisfaction but less than the first. The satisfaction keeps dropping, and overconsumption gives negative utility.

</TopicSection>

---

<TopicSection id="dmu-assumptions" title="11. Assumptions of the law of diminishing marginal utility">

<KeyIdea>
The law holds only under conditions such as identical items, a rational consumer and constant taste.
</KeyIdea>

The lecture lists some of the assumptions (not all):

1. The items consumed are identical (you may not find the dip if the items are not identical, e.g. the same ice cream each time).
2. The units are consumed quickly, with few breaks in between.
3. The consumer can make rational decisions (can think and decide about consumption).
4. The items can be measured.
5. The consumer's taste remains constant.
6. The units consumed are neither too big nor too small.

</TopicSection>

Stores lower the price per unit for more units because of diminishing marginal utility.
</KeyIdea>

- Store pricing for a backpack (INR): one for ₹300, two for ₹550, three for ₹750.
- As volumes go up, the price per unit comes down. Economies of scale and scope come in the next video.

| 3 | ₹750 | 750 / 3 = ₹250 |

<AddedByTutor>
The price per backpack column is my arithmetic. The lecture gives only the totals and says the consumer gets the best price per backpack by choosing the last option.

- The consumer gets the best price per backpack with the last choice. In reality, not all buyers want three backpacks even for a good deal; most just want one.
- The first backpack is always priced higher, because anyone buying a backpack needs at least one.
- To persuade customers to buy more, the company must decrease the cost per unit. If prices stay the same, then as you buy more backpacks the marginal utility comes down (diminishing marginal utility), and customers do not buy more.

<TopicSection id="mu-mc" title="13. Marginal utility equals marginal cost">

<KeyIdea>
At the point where marginal utility equals marginal cost, extra consumption gives no further satisfaction.
</KeyIdea>

- As the quantity consumed increases, the utility reduces, and the marginal cost increases.
- Where they intersect, utility equals cost, and the customer no longer derives satisfaction from additional consumption.

$$\text{MU} = \text{MC}$$

- These problems are solved in the live session.

<TopicSection id="rationality" title="14. Rationality and cost-benefit analysis">

<KeyIdea>
People and organizations choose when the benefits outweigh the costs, though fewer alternatives can change this.
</KeyIdea>

> **Definition (from the lecture):** Human beings are rational animals: decisions are based on a thought process (rationalism), though some are impulse buys.

> **Cost-benefit analysis (from the lecture):** Businesses or individuals assess costs and benefits and tend to make choices when benefits outweigh costs, and may not when costs outweigh benefits.

$$\text{Benefits} > \text{Costs} \implies \text{choose}$$

- **Organizational buying (week 3 topic):** to choose between two or three vendors or suppliers, an organization uses assessment criteria (called critical or key success factors), such as pricing, service, product quality, service quality and brand image. It adds weightage, rates each option, and picks the highest rating. This varies across organizations.

<CheckBadge reason="The transcript has the garbled phrase 'assessment capsticks, which are called as critical or key success factors'; I read it as assessment criteria." />

- **It is not always so:** availability of alternatives matters.
  - B2C: the customer has many choices and can easily switch brands, so the switching cost (cost of moving from one brand to another) is lower.
  - B2B: fewer alternatives, so the switching costs are higher.
- In a monopoly, where alternatives are few, the cost-benefit logic may not hold good.

</TopicSection>

<TopicSection id="opportunity-cost" title="15. Opportunity cost">

<KeyIdea>
Every choice means giving up other choices, and the cost of those lost opportunities is the opportunity cost.
</KeyIdea>

> **Definition (from the lecture):** When consumers make a choice, they must give up (sacrifice or reject) the other choices. The cost associated with such lost opportunities is the opportunity cost.

> **Plain language:** The price of choosing one thing is what you could have done with the money or time instead.

- The instructor quotes Dan Ariely: every time you spend money on something, you cannot spend it on something else. Resources are scarce.

<CheckBadge reason="The instructor is unsure how to describe Dan Ariely (said 'Israeli-American, I think he's an economist'); the attribution is kept as stated." />

**Grocery example**
- Near your home a store sells groceries at MRP (maximum retail price). A supermarket (D-Mart, Reliance Fresh or Spencer's) is a 15 to 20 minute drive away.
- Buy nearby: the extra money you paid could have been used for something else, which is the opportunity cost.
- Drive to the supermarket: the time spent reaching it could have been used for something else, which is the opportunity cost.
- The decision is a subjective cost-benefit assessment that differs from person to person. Someone may avoid wasting time and pay the extra money; another may go for the discount, variety and better ambience.

**Other examples**
- The instructor spent 150 hours preparing and recording the BDM theory lectures; the opportunity cost is what else those 150 hours could have been used for.
- Paying ₹1,000 to watch a movie: the ₹1,000 could have been used for something else.
- **Produce vs outsource (businesses):** producing costs extra time or resources that could be used elsewhere; outsourcing costs extra money that could be used elsewhere.
- Every company has an objective: maximize utility, minimize cost or enhance savings. Companies do mathematical modeling (cost-benefit analysis is one) and decide on the option closest to the chosen objective.

</TopicSection>
