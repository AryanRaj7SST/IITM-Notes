<TopicSection id="needs-wants-desire" title="1. Needs, wants and desire">

<KeyIdea>
Needs are essential for survival, wants improve quality of life once needs are met, and desire is a want you cannot afford.
</KeyIdea>

- This first video gives an overview of economics. It does not go into depth and covers only some key concepts.

| Term | Lecture meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Need | Essential for survival and well-being; one cannot survive without these basic requirements | Food, clothing, shelter, healthcare |
| Want | Appears once basic needs are fulfilled and our aspiration goes up; enhances the quality of life | Entertainment, travel |
| Desire | The only difference from a want is affordability: we wish to buy something but may not have enough funds | Items we wish to buy but cannot afford |

> **Plain language:** A need keeps you alive, a want makes life better, and a desire is a want you cannot afford yet.

- The lecture also says a want can be thought of as a specific need, and that we think of wants only once our basic needs are fulfilled.

<CheckBadge reason="Food, clothing and shelter are first given as needs, and later in topic 4 they appear again in the list of wants; both statements are kept as spoken." />

</TopicSection>

<TopicSection id="infinite-wants" title="2. Infinite (insatiable) wants">

<KeyIdea>
Human beings are never satisfied with what they have and always want more.
</KeyIdea>

> **Definition (from the lecture):** Human beings have infinite wants, also called insatiable wants.

> **Plain language:** However much we have, we always want more.

- Once our basic needs are fulfilled, we have wants, and all human beings have infinite wants.
- This is a universal belief among economists (those who study economics) and among people in general.
- It is not based on any evidence or scientific testing; people simply assert that it is true.

</TopicSection>

---

<TopicSection id="selfish" title="3. Are human beings selfish?">

<KeyIdea>
Infinite wants do not make people selfish, because people also wish others to have more.
</KeyIdea>

- Question: does having infinite wants mean human beings are selfish? The answer is not necessarily.
- Everybody has infinite wants, and human beings also think of others, are concerned about others, and wish others to have more.
- The lecture says this shows the positive side of human beings.

</TopicSection>

---

<TopicSection id="goods-services" title="4. Wants as goods and services">

<KeyIdea>
Wants are the goods and services that individuals desire, and they are produced using resources.
</KeyIdea>

> **Definition (from the lecture):** Wants are the goods and services that individuals desire, such as food, clothing, shelter and anything else that enhances the quality of life.

> **Plain language:** Wants are the things and services people would like to have.

- The difference between product and service is covered in week 3.
- The next question is how to produce these goods and services: for that we need **resources**.

</TopicSection>

---

<TopicSection id="scarcity" title="5. Scarce resources and constraints">

<KeyIdea>
Resources are scarce, so there are limits on what can be produced and which wants can be satisfied.
</KeyIdea>

> **Definition (from the lecture):** Scarce means short in supply, which means there is a shortage.

> **Plain language:** There is never enough of the resources to make everything people want.

- Human wants are unlimited or infinite, but the resources to meet them are scarce (less).
- Scarcity of resources implies **constraints**: limitations on the goods and services that can be produced.
- Goods and services are produced by resources, so resource limitations also limit what can be produced and which human wants can finally be satisfied.

</TopicSection>

---

<TopicSection id="definition" title="6. Definition of economics">

<KeyIdea>
Economics is the science of constrained choices: allocating scarce resources to meet unlimited human wants.
</KeyIdea>

> **Definition (from the lecture):** Economics is the science of constrained choices. It is the science that deals with the allocation of scarce resources to meet unlimited human wants. (This is the first definition given.)

> **Plain language:** Economics is about choosing how to use limited resources when people want unlimited things.

</TopicSection>

---

<TopicSection id="economic-free-goods" title="7. Economic goods and free goods">

<KeyIdea>
Economic goods are scarce, free goods have enough supply; not paying for something does not make it a free good.
</KeyIdea>

Economists classify goods into two types:

| Type | Lecture meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Economic goods | Goods that are scarce in nature; most of the resources used to produce goods are always scarce | Goods produced from scarce resources |
| Free goods | Goods with enough supply to meet human wants | Air, available free in supply |

- Question: if people do not pay for a good, is it a free good? Not necessarily.
- Example: a government program of free hospitalization for poor people means they do not pay hospitalization charges or for medical facilities. It is not a free good. It is an **economic good**, because scarce resources are allocated to provide healthcare.

</TopicSection>

---

<TopicSection id="factors-of-production" title="8. Resources and the four factors of production">

<KeyIdea>
Land, labor, capital and enterprise combine to produce goods and services.
</KeyIdea>

- Resources include land, labor, raw materials and so on. We get them from Mother Earth, where we live. They are always scarce, while human wants are infinite.
- Economists divide the earth's scarce resources into **four factors of production**.

| Factor | Lecture meaning |
|---|---|
| Land | Scarce resources, such as oil or sea |
| Labor | People available to work, with knowledge, intelligence and so on |
| Capital | Goods used to produce other goods, for example buildings and machinery |
| Enterprise | Entrepreneurs who combine the other three factors (land, labor and capital) to produce goods and services |

<CheckBadge reason="Land is described as scarce resources 'such as oil or sea' (as spoken); kept as stated." />

> **Definition (from the lecture):** Production is any activity carried out that satisfies a human want.

> **Plain language:** Whenever someone makes something or does something that meets a want, that is production.

- Land plus labor plus capital plus enterprise, when combined, results in the production of goods and services that meet human wants. When those goods and services are supplied, production has taken place.

</TopicSection>

---

<TopicSection id="economic-problem" title="9. The economic problem">

<KeyIdea>
Wants are infinite but resources are finite, so scarce resources must be carefully allocated.
</KeyIdea>

> **Definition (from the lecture):** The economic problem arises because human wants are infinite while the resources needed to meet them are finite (scarce), so we cannot get whatever we want and must carefully allocate scarce resources.

> **Plain language:** We cannot have everything, so we must choose how to use what we have.

- Economics is all about tackling this issue.

</TopicSection>

<TopicSection id="myths" title="10. Myths: what economics is not">

<KeyIdea>
Economics is not just money, and knowing it does not guarantee business success.
</KeyIdea>

| Myth | What the lecture says |
|---|---|
| Economics is money | Economists do study money as a topic, but only because it addresses the economic problem |
| Business success is guaranteed if you know economics | Knowing economics makes you a better salesperson, but success is not guaranteed. Studying it neither makes you rich nor poor. It helps you understand business scenarios and make decisions accordingly |

</TopicSection>

<TopicSection id="micro-macro" title="11. Microeconomics and macroeconomics">

<KeyIdea>
Macro studies the entire economy top-down, micro studies individuals and firms bottom-up, and both rest on constrained choice.
</KeyIdea>

Economics has two branches.

| | Macroeconomics | Microeconomics |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | The larger economy, the entire economy | Smaller parts of the economy |
| Approach | Top-down | Bottom-up |
| Tariff example | Impact of Trump tariffs on India, on the employment rate and on GDP | Impact of the tariffs on consumer behavior, individuals, businesses and workers |
| Other example | A government allocates a lot of budget to education, so it may allocate less to infrastructure | A consumer who spends a lot of time working may spend less time on leisure |

- They are not two different subjects; they are interconnected.
- Constrained choice or optimization is present in both macro and micro, and it is up to the decision maker to make the best decision under the circumstances.
- The next video discusses the difference between macroeconomics and microeconomics in more detail, with focus on microeconomic concepts relevant to the business data management project.

</TopicSection>
