<TopicSection id="supervised-framing" title="1. Supervised Learning Formulation & Notations">

<KeyIdea>
Supervised regression seeks a parameterized hypothesis $h_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{x})$ that maps $d$-dimensional feature vectors to real-valued scalar targets $y \in \mathbb{R}$ by minimizing prediction discrepancy.
</KeyIdea>

In supervised regression, our training dataset consists of $n$ independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) observations:

$$\mathcal{D} = \{ (\mathbf{x}^{(1)}, y^{(1)}), (\mathbf{x}^{(2)}, y^{(2)}), \dots, (\mathbf{x}^{(n)}, y^{(n)}) \}$$

Where each input instance $\mathbf{x}^{(i)}$ is a column vector in $\mathbb{R}^d$:

$$\mathbf{x}^{(i)} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1^{(i)} \\ x_2^{(i)} \\ \vdots \\ x_d^{(i)} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^d, \quad y^{(i)} \in \mathbb{R}$$

To account for the intercept (bias term $w_0$) seamlessly without writing separate terms, we append an auxiliary constant feature $x_0 = 1$ to every vector. This is known as the **augmented feature vector**:

$$\mathbf{x}^{(i)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ x_1^{(i)} \\ \vdots \\ x_d^{(i)} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{d+1}, \quad \mathbf{w} = \begin{bmatrix} w_0 \\ w_1 \\ \vdots \\ w_d \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{d+1}$$

Our hypothesis function is therefore a linear dot product:

$$h_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x} = w_0 + w_1 x_1 + \dots + w_d x_d$$

<AddedByTutor>
Notice the convention: in modern linear algebra literature, vectors are column vectors by default unless transposed. Thus $\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x}$ produces an inner product resulting in a scalar.
</AddedByTutor>

</TopicSection>

<TopicSection id="loss-and-cost" title="2. The Mean Squared Error (MSE) Cost Surface">

<KeyIdea>
The Mean Squared Error cost function computes the average squared euclidean distance between predictions and ground truth, forming an unconstrained convex paraboloid.
</KeyIdea>

For an individual observation $i$, the residual (error) is defined as:

$$e^{(i)} = h_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}) - y^{(i)}$$

Squaring this residual penalizes larger deviations quadratically while ensuring non-negativity. Summing over all $n$ samples gives our cost objective:

$$J(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{2n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( h_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}) - y^{(i)} \right)^2$$

### Vectorized Matrix Formulation

Rather than iterating with summations, high-performance implementations construct the **Design Matrix** $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (d+1)}$:

$$\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} - & (\mathbf{x}^{(1)})^T & - \\ - & (\mathbf{x}^{(2)})^T & - \\ & \vdots & \\ - & (\mathbf{x}^{(n)})^T & - \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_1^{(1)} & \dots & x_d^{(1)} \\ 1 & x_1^{(2)} & \dots & x_d^{(2)} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 1 & x_1^{(n)} & \dots & x_d^{(n)} \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} y^{(1)} \\ y^{(2)} \\ \vdots \\ y^{(n)} \end{bmatrix}$$

The entire prediction vector across all $n$ examples is simply $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}$. Hence, the residual vector is:

$$\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$$

The cost function $J(\mathbf{w})$ can now be written in clean Euclidean norm format:

$$J(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{2n} \| \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y} \|_2^2 = \frac{1}{2n} (\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y})^T (\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y})$$

</TopicSection>

<TopicSection id="ols-derivation" title="3. Step-by-Step Derivation of the Normal Equation">

<KeyIdea>
Because $J(\mathbf{w})$ is quadratic and convex, the global minimum occurs precisely where the gradient vector $\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} J(\mathbf{w})$ equals the zero vector $\mathbf{0}$.
</KeyIdea>

<Collapsible title="Full Matrix Calculus Derivation (Step-by-Step)" defaultOpen={true}>

Let us expand the quadratic form step by step:

$$
\begin{aligned}
J(\mathbf{w}) &= \frac{1}{2n} (\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y})^T (\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}) \\
&= \frac{1}{2n} \left( (\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w})^T - \mathbf{y}^T \right) (\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}) \quad &&\text{[Transpose of difference: } (A - B)^T = A^T - B^T\text{]} \\
&= \frac{1}{2n} \left( \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X} \mathbf{w} - \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{y}^T \mathbf{X} \mathbf{w} + \mathbf{y}^T \mathbf{y} \right) \quad &&\text{[Distributive property of matrix multiplication]}
\end{aligned}
$$

Note that $\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{y}$ is a $1 \times 1$ scalar. For any scalar $s$, $s^T = s$. Therefore:

$$
(\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{y})^T = \mathbf{y}^T (\mathbf{X}^T)^T (\mathbf{w}^T)^T = \mathbf{y}^T \mathbf{X} \mathbf{w}
$$

The two middle terms are identical! Combining them gives:

$$
J(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{2n} \left( \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X} \mathbf{w} - 2 \mathbf{y}^T \mathbf{X} \mathbf{w} + \mathbf{y}^T \mathbf{y} \right)
$$

Now, we take the gradient with respect to $\mathbf{w}$ using matrix calculus identities:

1. $\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} (\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{A} \mathbf{w}) = 2 \mathbf{A} \mathbf{w}$ when $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X}$ is symmetric.
2. $\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} (\mathbf{b}^T \mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{b}$ where $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{y}$.
3. $\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} (\mathbf{y}^T \mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{0}$ because it does not depend on $\mathbf{w}$.

Applying these identities:

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} J(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{2n} \left( 2 \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X} \mathbf{w} - 2 \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{y} \right) = \frac{1}{n} \left( \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X} \mathbf{w} - \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{y} \right)
$$

Setting $\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} J(\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{0}$:

$$
\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X} \mathbf{w} - \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X} \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{y}
$$

Assuming $\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X}$ is invertible (full column rank), multiply both sides from the left by $(\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X})^{-1}$:

$$
\mathbf{w}^* = (\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{y}
$$

</Collapsible>

<AddedByTutor>
**SymPy Verification**: We verified this symbolic differentiation in Python (`sympy.MatrixSymbol`). The Hessian matrix is $\nabla^2_{\mathbf{w}} J(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{n} \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X}$. Since $\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X}$ is positive semi-definite (and strictly positive definite when $\mathbf{X}$ has full column rank), the second derivative test confirms $\mathbf{w}^*$ is a strict global minimum.
</AddedByTutor>

</TopicSection>

<TopicSection id="gradient-descent" title="4. Batch Gradient Descent & Hyperparameter Tuning">

<KeyIdea>
Batch Gradient Descent iteratively adjusts parameters in the opposite direction of the gradient vector, guaranteed to converge to the global minimum on convex surfaces with an appropriate step size.
</KeyIdea>

While the Normal Equation provides an exact analytical solution, computing $(\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X})^{-1}$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^3)$ operations. When $d > 10,000$, matrix inversion becomes impractical.

Gradient Descent starts with an initial guess $\mathbf{w}^{(0)}$ (typically zeros or small random values) and updates iteratively:

$$\mathbf{w}^{(t+1)} = \mathbf{w}^{(t)} - \eta \nabla_{\mathbf{w}} J(\mathbf{w}^{(t)})$$

Substituting our gradient:

$$\mathbf{w}^{(t+1)} = \mathbf{w}^{(t)} - \frac{\eta}{n} \mathbf{X}^T (\mathbf{X} \mathbf{w}^{(t)} - \mathbf{y})$$

### Geometric Intuition & Contour Surface

The plot below illustrates the trajectory of Batch Gradient Descent navigating the elliptical cost contours toward the global optimum $\mathbf{w}^* = (2.0, 3.0)$:

![Gradient Descent Loss Contours](/images/mlt/gradient_descent_contour.svg)

### Learning Rate ($\eta$) Dynamics:
- **$\eta$ too small**: Convergence is agonizingly slow, requiring thousands of unnecessary epochs.
- **$\eta$ optimal**: Smooth, exponential convergence toward $\mathbf{w}^*$.
- **$\eta$ too large**: Overshoots the basin, oscillating wildly or diverging to numerical infinity.

</TopicSection>
